# GLP-1 Clients Are Losing Muscle They Can't Afford To. Here's The Protocol That Stops It.

I had a 47-year-old recreational lifter come in after 12 weeks on semaglutide. She'd lost 9 kg. Her DEXA showed 3.8 kg of that was lean mass. Her squat had dropped 15 kg. She was eating roughly 1,100 calories a day and doing three full-body sessions a week — a program that was perfectly adequate at maintenance but nowhere near sufficient in a 600-calorie deficit.

Here's what we changed, and what the evidence says about why it worked.

## The scale weight lie

GLP-1 receptor agonists — semaglutide, tirzepatide, liraglutide — are remarkably effective for fat loss. The STEP 1 trial (Wilding et al. 2022) demonstrated 14.9% mean body weight reduction over 68 weeks in adults with obesity (BMI ≥30) on 2.4 mg semaglutide weekly, compared to 2.4% in placebo. That's a massive absolute drop.

But the composition of that loss is where coaches get into trouble.

Astejada et al. (2024) conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of body composition changes during GLP-1-based therapies. Across 13 included studies, lean mass accounted for a substantial proportion of total weight lost — ranging from roughly 20-40% depending on the population, intervention duration, and whether structured resistance training was included. In the STEP 1 trial specifically, approximately 39% of the weight lost was lean mass in the absence of a standardized exercise protocol.

Let me be direct about what that means: if your GLP-1 client loses 10 kg and you haven't aggressively managed their training and protein, roughly 3.5–4 kg of that loss may be contractile tissue they'll struggle to regain.

For a gen-pop weight loss client, that's aesthetically frustrating and metabolically counterproductive. For a masters athlete or anyone with existing sarcopenic risk, it's a genuine functional problem.

## The preservation protocol: what the evidence supports

What I've found works in practice — and what the literature backs — is a three-pillar approach. I'm going to give you the exact programming I use for GLP-1 clients in a ≥25% caloric deficit.

### Pillar 1: Volume floor of ≥10 weekly sets per muscle group

Schoenfeld et al. (2017) established a dose-response relationship between weekly training volume and hypertrophy in trained individuals, with 10+ sets per muscle group per week producing superior gains to lower volumes. The meta-analysis showed diminishing returns beyond ~20 sets, but the lower bound is the relevant number here.

In a caloric deficit, your client does not have the recovery resources to tolerate high-end volume. But they absolutely cannot afford to drop below the effective dose threshold. The common coach instinct to "reduce volume to aid recovery" in a deficit is well-intentioned but backwards for muscle preservation.

**Protocol:**
- 10–14 sets per muscle group per week
- Split across 2 sessions per muscle group (5–7 sets each)
- Upper/lower or push-pull-legs split, 4 days per week
- This is a floor, not a target. If they're recovering poorly, intensity is what you adjust — not volume.

For my 47-year-old client, we moved her from 3 full-body sessions (roughly 6 sets per muscle group per week) to an upper/lower 4-day split at 12 sets per muscle group per week. Total training time increased by roughly 45 minutes weekly. It was non-negotiable.

### Pillar 2: Intensity at 65–72.5% 1RM with proximity to failure

This is where practitioners get confused. The general hypertrophy range of 30–80% 1RM (Schoenfeld et al. 2021) gives you broad theoretical latitude. In a deficit, you need to narrow that window.

Helms et al. (2014) demonstrated that in energy-restricted resistance-trained individuals, higher relative loads (heavy loads, ~80% 1RM) preserved lean mass and strength better than moderate loads during caloric restriction over a 6-week period. The study used 24 trained male volunteers in a 40% caloric deficit — an extreme condition, but the signal is directionally useful.

What I see in practice is that clients in large deficits on GLP-1s struggle with loads above ~75% because recovery is genuinely compromised. The sweet spot I've landed on is:

**Protocol:**
- 65–72.5% 1RM for working sets
- 2 RIR on the first set of each exercise, progressing to 0–1 RIR on final sets
- Rep ranges: 8–12 for compound movements, 10–15 for isolation work
- Rest periods: 2–3 minutes for compounds, 90 seconds for isolation
- No training to absolute failure more than once per week per movement pattern

This is heavy enough to retain mechanical tension and neural drive, light enough that the sets are completable without form breakdown in a depleted state.

### Pillar 3: Protein at 1.6–2.2 g/kg adjusted body weight

This isn't a training variable, but it's the third leg of the stool and I've watched coaches fail here repeatedly with GLP-1 clients.

The appetite suppression from GLP-1 agonists is profound. In my experience, clients often struggle to hit even 80g of protein daily without intentional planning. They're not hungry. The drug is doing exactly what it's supposed to do — reducing hedonic eating and gastric emptying. But that makes hitting the protein threshold an active logistical challenge, not a passive outcome.

Morton et al. (2018) conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis showing that protein supplementation during resistance training in a caloric deficit augmented lean mass gains and fat loss. The effect was most pronounced at intakes ≥1.6 g/kg/day.

**Protocol:**
- Target 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day using adjusted body weight (use target weight, not current weight, for clients with BMI ≥35)
- Front-load protein: 40–50g in the first meal, which is often the only meal they reliably eat
- Use hydrolyzed whey or essential amino acid supplements if solid food is unappealing — GLP-1 clients frequently report meat aversion and early satiety with dense protein sources
- Casein before bed if total daily intake is marginal

## Tempo and exercise selection

A quick note on these because they matter in a deficit more than at maintenance.

**Tempo:** 2-1-2-0 for compounds, 2-0-2-0 for isolation. Controlled eccentrics matter more when recovery is limited. Your client cannot afford to rely on elastic rebound or momentum when their glycogen is perpetually low.

**Exercise selection:** Keep it simple and stable. Machine-based movements are underrated here. The Smith machine, leg press, hack squat, and cable columns offer the stability to train close to failure safely when your client is fatigued from a 600+ calorie deficit. Barbell free-weight work is fine for the first movement of each session when they're freshest; subsequent exercises should bias stability.

I program 2 compound movements per session and 2–3 isolation movements. The compounds drive the intensity signal; the isolations drive the volume without the systemic fatigue cost.

## The regulatory and evidence-status disclaimer

GLP-1 receptor agonists are FDA-approved for type 2 diabetes (semaglutide, tirzepatide, liraglutide) and chronic weight management (semaglutide 2.4 mg, tirzepatide). The STEP and SURPASS trial programs provide robust human efficacy and safety data.

However, their off-label use in non-diabetic, non-obese populations for physique or performance purposes is not well-studied. The long-term effects on body composition in trained individuals under controlled exercise conditions are still being investigated. Much of what I've described above is practitioner inference from the general energy restriction literature (Helms et al. 2014; Schoenfeld et al. 2021) applied to the specific pharmacological context of GLP-1 use.

The Astejada et al. (2024) meta-analysis is one of the first to specifically examine lean mass outcomes across GLP-1 trials. We need more data in trained populations with standardized resistance training protocols. What I'm sharing here is my current best practice informed by available evidence and clinical observation — not a settled protocol with long-term trial backing.

What I see in my clients on this protocol is markedly better lean mass retention compared to their pre-intervention baselines. DEXA scans at 8–12 weeks consistently show lean mass loss below 20% of total weight lost, which compares favorably to the 30–40% seen in untrained GLP-1 cohorts in the literature.

## Putting it together: sample training week

For a GLP-1 client in a 25–30% caloric deficit, 4 training days per week:

**Day 1 — Upper A**
- Barbell bench press: 4 × 8 @ 2 RIR, 70% 1RM
- Cable row: 3 × 10 @ 1 RIR
- Overhead press (dumbbell): 3 × 10 @ 1 RIR
- Bicep curl (cable): 2 × 12 @ 0 RIR
- Tricep pushdown: 2 × 12 @ 0 RIR

**Day 2 — Lower A**
- Smith machine squat: 4 × 8 @ 2 RIR, 72.5% 1RM
- Romanian deadlift (dumbbell): 3 × 10 @ 1 RIR
- Leg press: 3 × 12 @ 1 RIR
- Seated leg curl: 2 × 12 @ 0 RIR
- Standing calf raise: 3 × 15 @ 0 RIR

**Day 3 — Rest or light walking**

**Day 4 — Upper B**
- Pull-up or assisted pull-up: 4 × 8 @ 2 RIR
- Incline dumbbell press: 3 × 10 @ 1 RIR
- Face pull: 3 × 15 @ 1 RIR
- Lateral raise (dumbbell): 3 × 15 @ 0 RIR
- Overhead tricep extension (cable): 2 × 12 @ 0 RIR

**Day 5 — Lower B**
- Hack squat: 4 × 8 @ 2 RIR, 70% 1RM
- Bulgarian split squat (dumbbell): 3 × 10/leg @ 1 RIR
- Lying leg curl: 3 × 12 @ 1 RIR
- Seated calf raise: 3 × 15 @ 0 RIR

**Days 6–7 — Rest**

Total weekly sets per muscle group: 10–14 depending on how you categorize overlapping patterns. Volume is distributed, intensity is managed, and exercise selection biases toward stability as fatigue accumulates within each session.

## The bottom line for coaches

GLP-1 clients are going to present in your practice with increasing frequency. The drug works for weight loss. The question is whether you, as the coach, are going to let them lose muscle they can't afford to lose — or whether you're going to adjust your programming to match the physiological reality of a 25%+ caloric deficit with pharmacological appetite suppression.

Raise volume to the effective floor. Keep intensity in the 65–72.5% range. Prioritize protein logistics like it's your job — because it is.

The EvoFit team has seen this protocol work across dozens of GLP-1 clients now. It's not magic. It's just applying what we already know about muscle preservation in energy restriction to a population that needs it more than most.
